# Zettelkasten

A slip box. The word is ordinary German for a card index and became a proper noun in English because of one particular box: the one Niklas Luhmann, the sociologist, kept from 1951 until his death in 1998. Two collections in fact, about ninety thousand slips between them, A6 paper, mostly handwritten, now digitised by the archive at Bielefeld. He credited it as his co-author and was only half joking: the essay he wrote about it in 1981 is titled "Communicating with slip boxes", and he meant communicating.

## Numbering

Each slip carries a number, and the number is where the slip lives, not what it is about. A new slip that continues the thought on slip 21/3 does not get 21/4; it gets 21/3a, and the one that continues *that* gets 21/3a1, and so on, letters and digits alternating, as deep as the thought goes. Slips 21/4 and onward are for other things. The result is a sequence that branches wherever it needs to and never has to be renumbered.

This is what people mean by *Folgezettel*, follow-on slips. The important part is not the scheme; it is that placing a slip forces a decision about what it follows from. The box is written into, not sorted afterwards.

## The index

Luhmann kept a keyword register, but a sparse one: one or two slip numbers per keyword, sometimes three, never a list of everything on the topic. The register gave you an entrance. From the slip you followed the references written on it to other slips, and from those to others. The topic was not in the index; it was in the walk.

He was explicit that the box's value came from this: because the numbering was fixed and the references were many, the box turned up neighbours he had not planned, and the surprise was the point. A box that only returns what you filed is a filing cabinet.

## What the popular version adds

Ahrens's book from 2017 is the reason most people have heard the word. It adds a workflow: fleeting notes (scribbles, thrown away within a day or two), literature notes (what a source says, in your words, with the reference), and permanent notes (one idea each, written as if for a stranger, linked into the box). It is sensible and it is not Luhmann; Luhmann's slips were often terse and he did not distinguish the kinds.

## Tree or rhizome

The numbering is a tree in the box and a rhizome in the references. Slip 21/3a1 is a child of 21/3a whether it likes it or not, but the references on it can point anywhere, and Luhmann said the references mattered more than the position. This is the same tension as a vault with folders and links, and I do not think it is resolved by picking one. See [[Rhizomes#Where the metaphor stops]].

## What I actually do

- One note per idea where I can manage it, one note per thing (a person, a place, a session) where I cannot
- Links written at the time of writing, not added in a review pass, because the review pass never happens
- A note with no links in or out is a slip that fell behind the box; [[Home]] exists so there is always at least one way in

## Capture, again

Fleeting notes go in the daily note for the day. If they are still interesting the next morning they become a note; if not they stay where they are and get found by search or not at all. Literature notes go in the research folder with the source in the frontmatter. Permanent notes are the research folder. Everything else is the campaign, which has its own rules, and which is where I found out that a note about a *thing* is easier to write and harder to link than a note about an *idea*.
