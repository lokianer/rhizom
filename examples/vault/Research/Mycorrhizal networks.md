# Mycorrhizal networks

A mycorrhiza is a root and a fungus living together. The fungus grows into or around the root, takes sugar from the plant, and gives back water and mineral nutrients, mostly phosphorus and nitrogen, that its hyphae can reach and the root cannot. Something like four fifths of land plant species do this. The two main kinds are *arbuscular* mycorrhizas, where the fungus grows inside the root cells and which most herbs and crops have, and *ectomycorrhizas*, where the fungus wraps the root tips in a sheath and which most temperate and boreal trees have.

A **common mycorrhizal network** is what you get when one fungal individual colonises the roots of two or more plants at once. Since fungi are not fussy about which tree they attach to, a single mycelium can in principle connect a birch to a fir to a seedling of either. That much is not in dispute. What flows along the connection, and whether it matters at the scale of a forest, is.

## What has been shown

The experiment everyone cites is Simard and colleagues in 1997: paper birch and Douglas fir seedlings in the field, labelled with different carbon isotopes, and a measurable net transfer of carbon from birch to fir, larger when the fir was shaded. The transfer went through the soil and the authors argued, reasonably, that the shared fungal network was the route. There is a long line of greenhouse work before and after showing that nutrients and, in some setups, signalling compounds can move between plants that share a fungus.

The overview article on the general topic is a fair summary of the field as it stood before the 2023 review: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycorrhizal_network>.

## What has not been shown

Karst, Jones and Hoeksema went back through the literature in 2023 and sorted the popular claims into three:

| Claim | What the field evidence supports |
| --- | --- |
| Networks are widespread in forests | Few studies have actually mapped a network in the field; most infer it |
| Resources move through networks and help seedlings | Results are mixed; roughly as many neutral or negative outcomes as positive ones |
| Mature trees send warning signals to their neighbours through the network | No peer-reviewed field study at the time of the review |

Their second finding is the one I keep returning to: the more a paper was cited, the more positive its summary of the evidence tended to become. They call this positive citation bias. The claim did not get stronger; the retelling did.

> This is not a debunking. They are careful to say the networks exist and that transfer has been demonstrated in some conditions. The argument is that the forest-scale story ran ahead of the data, and that we have been reading the story back into the citations.

## Why it is in this vault

Three things carry over to the notes and to the tool I am building to read them.

1. **The links are made by a third party.** Neither tree grows towards the other. The fungus connects them because it is there and hungry. In a vault, the third party is whoever writes the note and puts the bracket in; the two notes did not ask to be joined. This is the difference between this metaphor and the [[Rhizomes]] one, where the plant is its own network.
2. **The connection has a cost.** The fungus takes a fifth or more of the plant's photosynthate. A link is cheap to type and not free to maintain; every one is a promise that the target exists and says what you thought it said.
3. **The network is real and the story about it is bigger than the network.** This is the caution for anything that draws a graph of notes and calls it a picture of thought. It is a picture of brackets. See [[Graph visualisation#The hairball]].
