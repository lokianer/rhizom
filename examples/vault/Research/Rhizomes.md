# Rhizomes

A rhizome, in botany, is a stem that grows horizontally underground and sends up shoots and down roots along its length. Ginger, couch grass, bamboo, irises. Cut it anywhere and both pieces keep growing. There is no trunk, no centre and no privileged direction.

Deleuze and Guattari borrowed the word in the introduction to *A Thousand Plateaus* (French original 1980; the introduction had appeared on its own in 1976) as a figure for a way of thinking and organising that is not a tree. The tree, or "arborescent" model, has a root, a trunk and branches: one origin, hierarchy, each element with exactly one place. Genealogies, taxonomies, org charts and most filing systems are trees. The rhizome is their counter-image.

## The six principles

They give the rhizome six "approximate characteristics", which I list here from the Massumi translation because I keep misremembering them.

1. **Connection** and 2. **heterogeneity**: "any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be." Unlike a tree, which fixes an order, and unlike the tree diagrams of Chomskyan linguistics, which they attack by name, the rhizome links things of different natures: signs, bodies, institutions, weather.
3. **Multiplicity**: a rhizome has no unity to serve as a pivot; it is defined by the number of its dimensions, and it cannot gain a dimension without changing in nature. There is no "the" rhizome that you then connect.
4. **Asignifying rupture**: "A rhizome may be broken, shattered at a given spot, but it will start up again on one of its old lines, or on new lines." Their example is ants. Mine is couch grass in a vegetable bed.
5. **Cartography** and 6. **decalcomania**: the rhizome is a map, not a tracing. A tracing reproduces a structure that is assumed to exist already; a map is made, has multiple entrances, can be torn and reassembled. "The map is open and connectable in all of its dimensions."

> A rhizome has no beginning or end; it is always in the middle, between things, interbeing, *intermezzo*.

## What this is and is not

It is not an argument that trees are bad. They are explicit that there are knots of arborescence in rhizomes and rhizomatic offshoots in roots; the point is that the tree has been treated as the only legitimate shape of thought.[^1] Nor is it a recipe. The introduction is written rhizomatically, which is to say it is hard going and repays being read out of order.

For my purposes the useful bits are:

- Any note can link to any other, and the link is not a subordinate relation
- A collection of notes is not a structure that exists before the notes; it is what the links make
- Rupture is normal: deleting a note or a folder should not break the whole
- Maps, not tracings: the graph view should show what is actually connected, not a folder tree redrawn as circles

## Where the metaphor stops

The biological rhizome is still one plant. The philosophical one is meant to be radically decentred. A vault of thirty-seven notes, one of them a Home page, is neither: it has a front door, which is arborescent, and a great many cross-links, which are not. See [[Über die Wurzeln]] for the pencilled phrase that got me onto this and [[Mycorrhizal networks]] for the biological system that actually behaves the way the philosophical rhizome is supposed to.
