# Sinus-Milieus

The Sinus-Institut in Heidelberg has been sorting German society into *milieus* since the late 1970s: groups of people who share a way of life and a set of values, rather than an income bracket or an age band. The model is proprietary, revised every few years, and licensed to marketers, broadcasters and political parties, which is why the chart turns up in so many presentations. I am interested in the chart, not the marketing.

## What the chart shows

The picture is always the same shape. A rectangle with two axes.

- **Vertical: social status.** Upper and upper-middle at the top, middle in the middle, lower-middle and lower at the bottom. This is built from education, income and occupation, the usual three.
- **Horizontal: basic orientation.** From left to right, roughly, *tradition* (hold on, preserve), *modernisation* (have and enjoy; be and change) and *re-orientation* (do and experience; overcome limits). The labels shift between editions; the direction does not.

Inside the rectangle sit the milieus, ten of them in the 2021 version for Germany, drawn as rounded, irregular, overlapping blobs. Germans call it the potato chart, which is accurate. A milieu's position is its centre of gravity on the two axes; its size is roughly its share of the population; the overlaps are deliberate, because the boundaries between, say, the adaptive-pragmatic middle and the performers are fuzzy and the institute wants you to see that they are fuzzy. A person can sit in the overlap. The names in the version in front of me run from the *conservative-established* and the *post-materialists* at the top, through the *adaptive-pragmatic middle*, to the *precarious* and *consumer-hedonists* at the bottom, with the *expeditives* and *neo-ecologicals* on the right edge where the model puts what it thinks is coming next.

## Why the chart works

It has few things on it. Ten blobs, two axes, one label each, no numbers. The eye reads the whole picture in a second and then reads the overlaps, which is where the information is. A pie chart of the same data would show the shares and lose the neighbourhoods; a table would show both and be read by nobody.

The thought that came while sketching on the 16th: this works because it has few labels. A graph of a vault with thirty-odd notes can label every node and look like this chart. A graph with three thousand cannot, and the layout has to be designed for the second case and merely tolerate the first. I am putting that here rather than in [[Graph visualisation]] so it is written down once; that note links back to this heading.

## What it does not show

- Individuals. A milieu is a distribution, and the blob is its outline at some unstated contour
- Movement. The chart is a snapshot; people drift right and sometimes down over a life
- Anything outside the two axes. The institute knows this and sells the detail separately

## What I want from it

Soft, overlapping regions in a graph view that say *these notes hang together* without drawing a hard border round them. Folder as milieu, not folder as box.
